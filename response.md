> It would be: “Does this concentration of infrastructure increase distributed productive capacity more than it increases dependency on an incontestable choke point?”

This is the exact trade off thinking — “how much centralization am I willing to accept, in return for how much technological acceleration” — that I was talking about possibly needing to adopt. Instead of treating centralization as an ultimate bad or immoral thing, asking, essentially: is the positive liberty, in terms of the range of possible actions, and the leverage of those actions, and the production of the new, granted to people, by this technology, greater than the centralization of control which it allows?

> A generative black box is centralized or technically opaque, but it multiplies downstream agency, access, experimentation, and productive capacity. A sovereign chokepoint is centralized in a way that makes exit, substitution, replication, or meaningful competition increasingly impossible.

I would argue that there are many technologically accelerative technologies that are nevertheless sufficiently technically opaque and large scale such that they are necessarily difficult to replicate, for both knowledge and resource reasons; it must be difficult to meaningfully compete with, because it's hard to replicate and because of economies of scale and first mover advantages, and as a result it will be difficult to meaningfully substitute for, and as a consequence of all that, plus Illich's concept of "radical monopoly" (a technology whose adoption automatically makes not using it, or using alternatives, more difficult --- an example of this is technologies with network effects), it would be hard to exit. We see this with the internet, mobile smartphones (through which more and more even basic government services operate) and EUV lithography (only one company in the entire world can make the machines that do it, and only like three can competitively use them).

> I would say: advanced technological society inevitably requires concentrations of capacity, but the accelerationist question is whether those concentrations remain contestable, forkable, substitutable, and generative, or whether they become rent-extracting control centers.

I will say that this is still a valuable framing, but it really does raise the question of natural, and radical, monpolies, and what to do about them. How do you design a system that, like Boids, is able to act coherently to avoid or curtail these traps, without needing a top down commander or a suffocating consensus mechanism, but just through the local rules it operates by?

Which brings me back to the "directness" of the post-OaU rules. *That's* more what I meant: I'm looking for a rule-system that's composed of a set of simple, locally applicable rules, which can be applied in a distributed, lateral conflict resolution style, on the level of individuals, preferably more or less atemporally, which can produce market acceleration without destroying technological acceleration.

So yeah, I still wonder if this comes down to trading off technological vs market acceleration, as I've used those terms. Speaking of which, some tentative definitions: **technological acceleration**: accelerating technological advancement, deterritorializaton and decoding especially of things like place, culture, tradition, and identity, alienation, atomization, increasing productivity and possibility in terms of what can be done in a general sense, increasing intelligence and autonomy of machines, increasing speed. **Market (perhaps political would be a better word) acceleration**: the deterritorialization and decoding of decisionmaking power, hierarchy, tribute, debt, obligation, decentralization of economic production and distribution, etc.
